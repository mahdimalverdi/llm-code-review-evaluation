# Abstract

Large language models can generate code review comments that are fluent but unsupported, irrelevant, incorrect, non-actionable, or too low-value to justify reviewer attention. Existing evaluations increasingly move beyond lexical similarity, yet correctness, grounding, relevance, usefulness, workflow outcomes, and evaluator validity are often studied separately. This targeted structured literature review synthesizes 121 locally available full-text studies on LLM-based and automated code review and closely related evaluation methods. Each study was extracted through a uniform eleven-section record and mapped to five review questions, a framework-traceability objective, and a 12-item quality-appraisal instrument. We organize the evidence into core, supporting, and peripheral tiers and use thematic synthesis rather than pooling incompatible metrics. The corpus supports four findings. First, problematic comments span grounding, correctness, relevance, localization, actionability, value, context, workflow, and evaluator failures. Second, no single metric adequately represents review quality; relevance, usefulness, acceptance, and correctness remain distinct. Third, mitigation mechanisms such as filtering, retrieval, context gating, verification, and escalation introduce preservation, coverage, and cost trade-offs that are inconsistently reported. Fourth, context and LLM-based evaluators are themselves sources of measurement risk, including noise, inconsistency, bias, and adversarial manipulation. Based on these findings, we derive an operational taxonomy, annotation protocol, and trade-off-aware evaluation framework. Because the original database-specific retrieval and screening history was not preserved, the review is reported as a targeted structured review rather than a fully reproducible systematic literature review.
